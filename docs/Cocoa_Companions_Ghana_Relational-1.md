# Cocoa Companions in Ghana: Trees, Crops, and the Relationships Between Them

## A note before the evidence

What gets called "companion planting" or "permaculture" in much of the world today is, in West Africa and many other places, simply how people have related to land for generations — planting food, medicine, timber and shade together because they belong together, not because a design system said so. This document uses the language of "evidence" and "species" because it draws on published research and institutional records, and because this work needs something that holds up to scrutiny. But the underlying relationships it describes — cocoa growing in the company of Ofram, Odum, plantain, Gliricidia — are relational planting practiced by Ghanaian farmers long before "evidence-based" was a phrase anyone needed.

**A note on terms.** Across much of West and East Africa, growing several crops and trees together on the same land is commonly called **mixed planting** or **mixed farming** — that's the term used in the reviewed literature itself, alongside "cocoa agroforestry." This document also uses **relational planting**, because it centres the relationships between cocoa and its companions — what each gives and asks of the other — rather than reducing the practice to a list of species or a technical planting pattern.

Local and Ghanaian names are given first throughout, with the Latin name in brackets as an identification reference only.

**Where to look for what.** Sections 3–8 are the direct working material for relational/mixed/companion planting decisions — the relationships (§3), and the species themselves organised by role (§4–8). Sections 1, 2 and 9–13 are supporting background — where the evidence comes from, how canopy layers are structured, and how to weigh and record a decision — useful for going deeper, but not needed to find a specific tree.

A small number of trees in §7.2, §8.1, §8.2 and §8.3 came into the original document under Latin name only. Their local Ghanaian names have been added here from separate botanical and ethnobotanical sources (Burkill's *Useful Plants of West Tropical Africa*, KNUST/Ghana ethnobotanical surveys, and related literature) — not from the [S1]–[S29] evidence base itself. Two species, *Cola chlamydantha* and *Canthium glabriflorum*, had no distinct Ghanaian vernacular name in the sources checked, so they're marked as such rather than guessed at.

## Scope and how the evidence is weighed

This document draws together the reviewed Ghana and West African evidence on choosing companion trees, shade trees and intercrops for cocoa farms in Ghana — COCOBOD/CRIG guidance (Ghana's own cocoa research and support institutions), Ghana-specific peer-reviewed research, CSIR-Ghana research, Bunso Cocoa College field evidence, West African institutional literature, and evidence from other cocoa-growing regions where it's genuinely useful.

### How the evidence is held

- **Strong Ghana evidence:** current COCOBOD/CRIG guidance or peer-reviewed research conducted in Ghana.
- **Ghana field-trial evidence:** a species was deliberately included in a Ghana cocoa field trial. Being included in a trial doesn't by itself mean a species outperforms others.
- **Ghana farmer-knowledge evidence:** documented farmer experience, preference or observed species traits in Ghana. These are lived observations, not laboratory measurements, unless a study specifically measured them.
- **Historical CRIG evidence:** species or practices named in older CRIG desirable/undesirable lists. Worth knowing, but not automatically today's guidance — many of these older classifications came out of colonial-era plantation science and deserve a second look rather than blind inheritance.
- **West African supporting evidence:** research from neighbouring cocoa-growing communities, especially Côte d'Ivoire.
- **Non-Ghana regional evidence:** evidence from cocoa systems elsewhere — useful for understanding mechanisms and structure, not sufficient on its own for what to plant in Ghana.
- **Inference:** a pattern drawn from the reviewed evidence, not a published COCOBOD/CRIG rule.

A central finding across the evidence: **there is no fixed, universal "good companion."** What works shifts with cocoa age, rainfall, soil, existing canopy, how intensively the farm is managed, disease pressure, and what matters most to the farmer. Farmers in a drier cocoa zone placed more value on fruit and income trees; farmers in a wetter zone favoured tall, cocoa-compatible timber trees more highly. [S1]

## 1. Where this evidence comes from

COCOBOD and the Cocoa Research Institute of Ghana (CRIG) are Ghana's own cocoa research and extension bodies. Current COCOBOD material distinguishes **temporary shade** from **permanent shade**, describing cocoa systems that deliberately include shade trees, fruit trees and other crops alongside cocoa. [S3] [S6]

Bunso Cocoa College is a COCOBOD training institution — not a CSIR research institute — though its farm has also hosted field research. [S18] Two of the Ghana cocoa-*Gliricidia sepium* sites in the Kaba et al. nitrogen-fixation study were on Bunso's experimental farm. [S20] Bunso also trains people in plantain, cassava, maize and cocoyam cultivation; that course list is not by itself evidence that all four are recommended cocoa companions — that claim needs cocoa-specific evidence. [S29]

CSIR's Plant Genetic Resources Research Institute (CSIR-PGRRI) is also based at Bunso and is a separate body from Bunso Cocoa College. [S19] CSIR-FORIG and CSIR-PGRRI contribute important Ghana-specific evidence on shade-tree traits, farmer preference, participatory agroforestry trials, crown cover and indigenous fruit-tree use. [S22] [S23] [S24] [S26] [S28]

## 2. The layers of a cocoa system

No COCOBOD or CRIG source sets out a single official Ghana-wide naming system for canopy layers. The Ghana evidence instead points to a functional pattern:

1. **Temporary shade** — especially plantain, while cocoa is being established or brought back into production. [S3]
2. **Food and early companions** — plantain, cocoyam, cassava and other food crops that hold space during the early years, rather than staying on as permanent companions to mature cocoa. [S10] [S23]
3. **The cocoa layer itself**
4. **Permanent, emergent trees above** — economic, timber, fruit and forest trees kept or planted for long-term shade and everything else they offer. [S3] [S6]

A COCOBOD document draws a clear line between **plantain suckers giving temporary shade** and **economic tree seedlings giving permanent shade**. [S3]

Ghana research describes a CRIG interpretation of roughly **18 emergent trees at least 12 m tall per hectare**, aiming for about **30–40% permanent shade** — while warning that counting stems alone misses a lot, since species, trunk size and crown shape all change how much shade actually falls. [S4] A Ghana REDD+ report makes the same point: canopy cover matters more than tree counts. [S5] A landscape study across 90 one-hectare plots in ten districts found stem densities of roughly 16.2–22.8 per hectare produced crown cover ranging from about 5.8% to 16.3% — the same number of trees, quite different amounts of shade. [S28]

A useful (though non-Ghanaian) structural reference comes from Sonwa and colleagues' work in southern Cameroon, which sorts cocoa agroforests into rough height bands: cocoa and plantain in the lowest 0–5 m layer, fruit trees like avocado at 5–10 m, mid-canopy trees like *Albizia* and *Ficus* at 10–20 m, and emergent timber such as *Terminalia superba* above 20 m — over 70% of associated trees in that study rose above the cocoa canopy itself. [S2]

## 3. What makes a good companion — how cocoa and its neighbours relate

Ghanaian agronomic and farmer knowledge draws on several kinds of relationship when weighing whether a tree belongs alongside cocoa: how it shares light, how it shares water and soil, what pests or disease it might carry, what physical risks it poses, and what it offers the farmer and household. [S1] [S22]

| What's being related to | What's being noticed | What the evidence shows | How to work with it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shade amount and quality | Whether the crown gives just enough shade, not too much | One of the most consistently important relationships | Dense, low, unmanageable shade is a real caution |
| Canopy shape | Height, spread, openness, leaf size, branching, airflow | High, open crowns are generally favoured | Low, dense crowns can make a species a poor fit at close spacing |
| Crown cover | Actual shade cast, not just tree count | Same stem counts can cast very different amounts of shade | Read canopy cover or crown class where that data exists |
| Timing of leaf change | Evergreen or deciduous, and when leaves drop | Matters because shade needs shift with the season | Weigh in context rather than ruling a species out automatically |
| Soil moisture | Whether the tree conserves or draws down moisture | Especially important in drier zones | Trees that dry the soil heavily are a real caution |
| Roots below ground | Competition for water, nutrients, rooting space | Named explicitly in Ghana farmer and agronomic knowledge | A real caution where overlap is severe; no Ghana-wide numeric threshold exists |
| Soil fertility | Effect on nutrients and organic matter | Matters, but depends on species and site | Weigh case by case |
| Leaf fall and nutrient return | How much litter, how fast it breaks down, what it returns | Fast-breaking, nutrient-rich litter is generally welcomed | A supporting point in favour |
| Nitrogen fixing | Whether the tree actually fixes atmospheric nitrogen | Directly shown in Ghana for *Gliricidia sepium* | A genuine point in favour — don't assume it from family membership alone |
| Pests and disease | CSSVD, black pod, mirids, mistletoe, stem borers, rodents | Can outweigh otherwise strong traits | Current phytosanitary guidance may rule a species out |
| Physical risk | Branch drop, windthrow, harvest or logging damage | A recurring Ghana concern | Serious risk can make a species impractical |
| Air and moisture | Airflow and humidity under the canopy | Matters in wet areas and for disease | Dense, still canopy may need rejecting or heavy pruning |
| Wind | Shelter without adding too much shade or humidity | A genuine benefit | Rarely a reason to exclude a species on its own |
| Weeds | Shade and litter holding weeds back | A labour-saving benefit | Weighs in favour |
| Allelopathy | Whether the tree's chemistry affects cocoa or neighbours | Present in farmer knowledge; no established Ghana rule for excluding on this basis | A flag to watch, not an automatic no |
| Food, fruit, spice, timber or other use | What the household or farm gains directly | Often the deciding factor for whether farmers take a species up | Weighed separately from biological fit |
| Labour | Establishment, pruning, harvest, residue | Can make an otherwise good match impractical | A workability question |
| Marketability | Local demand, transport, storage, buyers | Matters for whether the relationship pays off | A workability question |
| Seedling access | Whether planting material is actually available | A practical limit | Can stop a good match from happening at all |
| Tree tenure | Whether the farmer can actually harvest and benefit | Especially important for timber in Ghana | A real precondition, not a footnote |

Graefe et al.'s Ghana study named appropriate shade, soil moisture and fertility, organic matter, fast leaf breakdown, favourable microclimate, weed suppression, wind protection and airflow as the things farmers valued — and named water/nutrient competition, physical damage, and links to black pod, mistletoe, CSSVD, mirids, stem borers and rodents as what farmers weighed against a tree. [S1] The Atwima study, tied to CSIR, documented how farmers weighed pest and disease hosting, rooting habit, above-ground competition, allelopathy and crown shape — attending closely to crown size, branch number, leaf size and density because of what they do to light, humidity and airflow beneath the tree. [S22]

### 3.1 Which relationships matter most?

No source gives a single validated Ghana-wide weighting. The relationships that come up again and again are:

1. **Light, shade and microclimate**
2. **Pest, disease and physical risk**
3. **Water and site fit**
4. **Soil and nutrients**
5. **What the farmer needs and can manage**

In an Osino District study, 55% of the reasons farmers gave for removing trees from established cocoa farms came down to reducing shade and improving the cocoa's microclimate. [S7] Evidence from neighbouring Côte d'Ivoire tells a similar story — high, open crowns and low water competition were favoured, while low crowns, heavy shade, competition and pest-hosting were named as drawbacks. [S7] A broader analysis found low-canopy shade trees more likely to lower cocoa yield, while taller crowns could add carbon without hurting yield — a useful pattern, though it doesn't replace species-specific Ghana evidence. [S8]

### 3.2 Roots

Farmers in the Atwima study distinguished shallow- from deep-rooted trees because shallow lateral roots were felt to compete with cocoa for water and nutrients. [S22] Much of what's recorded about individual species' roots is **farmer knowledge**, not measured root data — worth naming honestly rather than dressing up as laboratory fact:

- measured directly;
- drawn from the literature;
- reported by farmers;
- not known.

No Ghanaian source gives a universal numeric root-depth threshold. [S9] *Terminalia ivorensis* shows why a simple yes/no label falls short: farmers valued its high, wide crown and timber, while also describing it as relatively shallow-rooted and potentially competitive — both things can be true. [S22]

### 3.3 Nitrogen fixing

Belonging to the bean family (Fabaceae) isn't, on its own, evidence that a tree fixes nitrogen. The strongest Ghana-specific case is ***Gliricidia sepium***. Kaba et al. studied cocoa-gliricidia sites in Ghana, including two at Bunso Cocoa College, where pruned gliricidia branches were left as green mulch. Using the ¹⁵N natural-abundance method, they found roughly **22–50% of gliricidia leaf nitrogen** came from the air, with about **48% held in the shoots**. [S20] This shows real biological nitrogen fixation and a nutrient contribution through the pruned material — it does not by itself show how much of that nitrogen reaches the cocoa, or prove a yield increase. [S20] Separate CSIR-FORIG work on an eight-year-old cocoa-gliricidia system found notably more above-ground biomass and stored carbon where gliricidia was present — biomass and carbon evidence, not direct proof of higher bean yield. [S21]

### 3.4 Allelopathy

Ghana farmer knowledge names allelopathy as something worth watching. [S22] No Ghanaian or West African source treats it as grounds for a routine exclusion. Recent experimental work on extracts from selected shade trees exists but isn't enough to support a general blacklist. [S12]

## 4. Trees with strong, well-established Ghana evidence

### 4.1 Permanent upper-canopy, timber and shade trees

| Local name | Latin name (reference only) | Family | What it offers and what the evidence shows | Evidence held as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ofram / limba | *Terminalia superba* | Combretaceae | Permanent emergent timber shade; historically named CRIG-desirable; highly ranked by Ghana farmers; notably higher major-season cocoa yield than unshaded cocoa in the 2022 Ghana study | Ghana field-tested + historical CRIG [S7] [S14] |
| Emire / idigbo | *Terminalia ivorensis* | Combretaceae | Permanent emergent timber shade; historically named CRIG-desirable and highly ranked; evaluated in the 2022 study but not among the four with a statistically clear yield advantage | Ghana field-tested/observational + historical CRIG [S7] [S14] |
| Odum / iroko | *Milicia excelsa* | Moraceae | High-canopy timber; historically named CRIG-desirable; highly valued by Ghana farmers; notably higher major-season yield than unshaded cocoa in the 2022 study | Ghana field-tested + farmer preference [S7] [S14] |
| African mahogany | *Khaya ivorensis* | Meliaceae | Permanent timber/upper-canopy tree; notably higher major-season yield than unshaded cocoa in the 2022 Ghana study | Ghana field-tested [S14] |
| Spanish cedar / cedrela | *Cedrela odorata* | Meliaceae | Planted permanent timber shade; CRIG recorded good results in cocoa establishment; notably higher major-season yield than unshaded cocoa in the 2022 study | Ghana field-tested + CRIG evidence [S14] [S16] |
| Nyamedua / stoolwood | *Alstonia boonei* | Apocynaceae | Historically named CRIG-desirable; a common Ghanaian shade tree in the 2022 trial; no statistically clear yield advantage found in that study | Ghana field-tested + historical CRIG [S7] [S14] |
| Otie / African nutmeg | *Pycnanthus angolensis* | Myristicaceae | Historically named CRIG-desirable permanent shade tree; farmers describe a high crown and deep roots, alongside brittle branches | Historical CRIG + Ghana farmer evidence [S7] [S22] |

The clearest species-specific yield result in the reviewed evidence: cocoa growing with ***Cedrela odorata, Khaya ivorensis, Terminalia superba*** and ***Milicia excelsa*** showed notably higher major-season yields than unshaded cocoa in one Western Ghana field study. Worth taking seriously, not worth assuming holds everywhere in Ghana regardless of zone, inputs or spacing. [S14]

### 4.2 A tree that gives back through tending

**Gliricidia (Gliricidia sepium)** — Fabaceae

Managed, pruned shade that fixes nitrogen and returns green biomass and nutrients to the soil. Supported by direct Ghana field research, including at Bunso Cocoa College [S20], with further support from CSIR-FORIG biomass and carbon evidence [S21]. What the evidence doesn't yet show: an exact quantity of nitrogen reaching the cocoa, or a guaranteed yield increase.

| Local name | Latin name (reference only) | Family | Role | Evidence held as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plantain | *Musa × paradisiaca* / plantain group | Musaceae | Temporary shade while cocoa is being established or rehabilitated; also food and income | Current COCOBOD practice [S3] |
| Cocoyam | *Xanthosoma sagittifolium* | Araceae | Shade-tolerant food companion during the early establishment years | West African cocoa evidence + Ghana practice [S10] |

Plantain has the clearest current official role in Ghana. Cocoyam, cassava and maize are supported for the **establishment stage**, not for staying alongside mature cocoa indefinitely. Bunso Cocoa College teaching plantain, cassava, maize and cocoyam cultivation isn't itself evidence that all four belong as cocoa companions. [S29]

### 6.1 CRIG's mixed fruit-tree trial

CRIG tested cocoa alongside a mix of *Allanblackia parviflora*, *Ricinodendron heudelotii*, *Persea americana* and *Tetrapleura tetraptera*, against a control shade mix of *Terminalia ivorensis* and *Terminalia superba*. At 78 months, dry bean yield showed no statistically significant difference between treatments, though canopy, ground cover, biomass and carbon did differ; the researchers noted the best spatial arrangement still hadn't been worked out. [S9] These trees are best described as **Ghana field-tested and potentially good multipurpose companions** — not conclusively the best cocoa shade species.

- *Entandrophragma angolense*

## 8. Trees that call for caution

### 8.1 Trees with strong phytosanitary or historical warnings

| Local name | Latin name (reference only) | What raises concern | How to hold it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wild cola (no distinct Ghanaian vernacular name found in the sources reviewed) | *Cola chlamydantha* | Historically named CRIG-undesirable; named as a CSSV alternative host in extension material | A real disease caution — check current CSSVD guidance before including it [S7] |
| Onyina | *Ceiba pentandra* | Historically named CRIG-undesirable; documented CSSV alternative host; farmers report brittle branches, pest hosting, mechanical/logging risk | A real disease and mechanical caution [S7] [S22] |
| Watapuo | *Cola gigantea* | Historically named CRIG-undesirable; ranked among the least beneficial in later Ghana literature; farmers report prolific regrowth, slow-breaking litter, dry/hard soil | A real caution depending on site and how it's managed [S7] [S22] |
| Odwuma | *Musanga cecropioides* | Historically named CRIG-undesirable; ranked among the least beneficial; farmers report shallow roots, heavy water use, brittle branches | A clear Ghana caution [S7] [S22] |

CSSV host status needs careful reading. Modern reviews confirm wild forest hosts are biologically relevant to the virus's spread, though how important they are relative to cocoa-to-cocoa transmission is still unsettled. It helps to hold apart **confirmed host status**, **official removal guidance**, and **how much that host actually matters epidemiologically**, rather than treating them as one thing. [S13]

### 8.2 Historically named "undesirable," with the reasoning unclear

The historical CRIG list also includes:

- Ɔdadeɛ — *Adansonia digitata*
- Akye fufo — *Blighia sapida*
- Wild cola (no distinct Ghanaian vernacular name found in the sources reviewed) — *Canthium glabriflorum*
- Krupi — *Carapa procera*
- Atwere — *Lecaniodiscus cupanioides*
- Nyankama — *Myrianthus arboreus*

These were named undesirable without a clear, species-specific reason given in the reviewed material. Best held honestly as:

**Historically named undesirable by CRIG; the reasoning isn't clear in the evidence reviewed.** [S7]

No root, pest, allelopathy or water-use explanation should be invented for these without a separate source to back it up.

|---|---|---|

### 8.4 Neither clearly good nor clearly bad

Some trees carry evidence pulling both ways:

- ***Ricinodendron heudelotii***: real fruit/NTFP and income value, but branch drop and a heavy crown bring mechanical risk. [S9] [S22]
- ***Persea americana*** and citrus: valuable fruit and income trees, with local mistletoe or pest concerns. [S10]
- ***Cola nitida***: income value and commonly kept in some systems, alongside a historical undesirable label and reported insect concerns. [S17] [S7]
- ***Triplochiton scleroxylon***: real timber value and Ghana field testing, alongside water, branch, pest and logging risk. [S14] [S22]
- ***Milicia excelsa***: strongly valued and well-supported in Ghana, though neighbouring West African farmers report local mirid concerns. [S7]
- ***Terminalia ivorensis***: high, wide, valuable crown, alongside farmer reports of shallow-root competition. [S22]

A tree with evidence pulling both ways is best held as **genuinely mixed** — not forced into a simple good/bad box.

## 9. What shapes how a companion actually affects the land

A 2024 study led by CSIR-FORIG with IITA and CIRAD compared cocoa agroforestry and monoculture across different soil conditions and humid versus sub-humid environments in Ghana, looking at shade-tree traits and their relationship to soil and ecosystem outcomes. [S26] [S27] Initial soil quality explained much of what happened to the soil; shade-tree traits helped predict the broader ecosystem effects. [S26] [S27]

This points toward choosing companions in sequence — starting from the farm and its location, moving through cocoa stage, site conditions, what's already growing, and what's needed — rather than treating cocoa alone as reason enough to recommend any single fixed tree.

## 10. Ghana evidence and evidence from elsewhere

| What was found | Where it comes from | How it applies in Ghana |
|---|---|---|
| Plantain temporary shade + permanent economic shade trees | Current COCOBOD documents | Applies directly [S3] |
| ~18 emergent trees ≥12 m/ha, 30–40% shade | Ghana/CRIG interpretation in peer-reviewed research | Relevant, though crown area and species still matter [S4] |
| Historical desirable/undesirable lists | Historical CRIG material, reproduced in a West African review | Ghana-specific history — don't assume it's current policy [S7] |
| Different priorities in drier Akumadan vs wetter Asankragua | Peer-reviewed Ghana study | Direct evidence for adjusting by zone [S1] |
| Yield response of eight common shade trees | Three-year study, 10 farms, Western Ghana | Strong local evidence — don't assume it holds nationwide [S14] |
| CRIG fruit-tree mixture trial | Ghana | Ghana-specific; ideal arrangement still unresolved [S9] |
| Atwima farmer knowledge on traits and poor fits | Ghana | Strong local knowledge — hold apart from measured traits [S22] |
| CSIR-FORIG participatory trial species | Ghana | Trial inclusion, not proof of superiority [S23] |
| African star apple use in cocoa | Eastern Ghana | Farmer-use and income evidence [S24] [S25] |
| Functional-trait/soil-context study | Ghana | Direct support for choosing by context [S26] [S27] |
| 0–5, 5–10, 10–20, >20 m layers | Southern Cameroon | A useful structure, not a Ghana standard [S2] |
| High/open crowns and low competition favoured | West Africa regionally | Useful, still needs local checking [S7] |
| Global cocoa agroforestry effects | Multi-region analysis | Useful for thinking, not for ranking Ghana species [S11] |

The global analysis makes clear that agroforestry outcomes shift with ecosystem, soil, climate, management, planting material, markets, farmer knowledge and land-use history — and disease outcomes depend on the specific pathogen and microclimate. [S11] That's why evidence doesn't transfer cleanly from Latin America or Southeast Asia: frosty pod rot and witches' broom are different diseases from Ghana's CSSVD and black pod. A relationship observed with one disease shouldn't be assumed to hold for another. [S11]

Some trees used in Ghana cocoa systems — *Gliricidia sepium*, *Cedrela odorata*, *Persea americana* — originate outside West Africa. Their place here rests on documented Ghana or West African use and testing, not simply on where they're originally from. [S7] [S15] [S20]

---

## 11. A way of choosing companions

There's no single published COCOBOD/CRIG formula for this. What follows draws that pattern out of the literature.

**First — care and caution.** Set aside, for closer review, any tree with current phytosanitary removal guidance, a confirmed disease-host link relevant to CSSVD recovery work, physical risk that can't be managed, an unmanageable dense crown at the intended spacing, aggressive regrowth, no legal right for the farmer to harvest or benefit from it, or no real way to get planting material or manage it. A historical "undesirable" label alone is a flag to look closer, not an automatic no, unless current official guidance backs it up.

**Second — know the farm.** Cocoa stage (establishment, young, mature, old/rehabilitating); rainfall and temperature; soil condition; water balance; how much canopy is already there; disease pressure, especially CSSVD; and what's already growing nearby.

**Third — weigh the fit.** Expected height, crown shape and openness, leaf size and timing, whether the shade will be right rather than too much, root and water competition, litter and nutrient return, nitrogen fixing where it's genuinely shown, pest and disease links, physical/wind risk, how much pruning it needs, and how it sits with what's already there.

**Fourth — weigh what the farmer needs**, separately from biological fit: food, fruit, spice or timber value; local market demand; household use; labour; seedling access; how long until it pays off; tenure and harvest rights; and the farmer's own goals and appetite for risk.

**Fifth — hold the evidence honestly.** For every recommendation, keep track of where the evidence comes from, what kind it is, whether it was measured or reported, whether it's consistent or conflicting, and whether it applies to establishment, mature cocoa, or both.

---

## 12. How to label the evidence honestly

Rather than a flat "recommended," use language that says what kind of evidence backs a claim:

- **Official Ghana establishment practice**
- **Ghana field-tested**
- **Ghana participatory trial**
- **Ghana farmer practice**
- **Ghana farmer knowledge**
- **Historically named CRIG-desirable**
- **Historically named CRIG-undesirable**
- **West African neighbouring evidence**
- **Evidence pulling both ways**
- **Reasoning unclear**
- **Evidence from outside Ghana only**
- **No reliable source found**

For example:

> **Plantain:** Good as temporary shade during cocoa establishment or rehabilitation; official Ghana practice. Its role is temporary shade and early food/income, not a place in the permanent mature-cocoa canopy.

> **Ofram (*Terminalia superba*):** A strong permanent-shade/timber companion with direct major-season yield evidence behind it; final fit still depends on existing canopy, site and how the farm is managed.

> **Njangsa (*Ricinodendron heudelotii*):** A multipurpose fruit/NTFP companion with real Ghana evidence, but branch drop and a heavy crown call for careful spacing and management.

> **Wawa (*Triplochiton scleroxylon*):** Evidence pulling both ways. Real timber and field-use value, alongside Ghana farmer reports of water, mechanical and pest concerns.

---

## 13. What to keep on record for each tree

Where the evidence exists, keep: local/common name; Latin name; family; role in the cocoa system; cocoa stage it suits; expected height/layer; crown openness; canopy-cover effect; what's known about its roots and whether that's measured, literature-based, farmer-reported or unknown; water competition; litter and nutrient return; nitrogen fixing; allelopathy; pest links; disease-host status; mistletoe association; physical/branch risk; windbreak value; weed suppression; food/fruit/spice/timber value; labour and management needs; seedling access; market/household value; tenure and harvest rights; which agroecological zone the evidence comes from; whether it's Ghana-specific; what kind of evidence it is; how confident that evidence is; the source; and anything still unresolved.

---

## 14. What holds true across all of this

1. **There's no single universal "best cocoa companion" list for Ghana.** What fits depends on cocoa stage, site, soil, water, existing canopy, disease pressure and how the farm is managed. [S1] [S26]
2. **Ghana's cocoa agroforestry is best understood by function** — temporary shade and early companions, the cocoa layer, and permanent trees above. Sonwa's Cameroon height bands are a useful reference, not a Ghana standard. [S2] [S3]
3. **How much shade a crown actually casts matters more than how many trees are counted.** [S4] [S22] [S28]
4. **Pest, disease, water competition and serious physical risk can outweigh economic or shade benefits.** [S1] [S22]
5. **No source gives a validated Ghana-wide numeric weighting system or root-depth threshold.**
6. **Nitrogen fixing supports a case for a tree; it doesn't make the case on its own.** Direct Ghana evidence exists for *Gliricidia sepium*, but bean-family membership alone isn't enough. [S20]
7. **Plantain has the clearest official role as temporary shade** in young or re-established cocoa. [S3]
8. **The strongest direct Ghana yield evidence among permanent shade trees supports Cedrela (*Cedrela odorata*), African mahogany (*Khaya ivorensis*), Ofram (*Terminalia superba*) and Odum (*Milicia excelsa*)** in the specific Western Ghana study reviewed. [S14]
9. **Fruit and multipurpose trees should be weighed for both cocoa compatibility and what they give the farmer.** CRIG and CSIR evidence supports several candidates, but economic value doesn't override serious agronomic risk. [S9] [S22] [S24]
10. **Historical CRIG "undesirable" labels shouldn't turn into unexplained modern bans.** Hold the history, the current disease evidence, and what's still unresolved, separately. [S7]
11. **Farmer knowledge is valuable and deserves to be labelled honestly** — a reported deep root, pest link or soil effect shouldn't be presented as a measured botanical fact unless it was actually measured. [S22]
12. **A defensible way of choosing runs: farm and location → cocoa stage → site conditions → existing canopy → care and caution → what's needed → tree traits → farmer fit → an honestly labelled recommendation.**
