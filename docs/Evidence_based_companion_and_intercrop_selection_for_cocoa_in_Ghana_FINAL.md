# Evidence-based companion and intercrop selection for cocoa in Ghana

## Scope and evidence standard

This document consolidates the reviewed Ghana and West African evidence on selecting companion trees, shade trees and intercrops for cocoa farms in Ghana. It combines official COCOBOD/CRIG guidance, Ghana-specific peer-reviewed research, CSIR-Ghana research, Bunso Cocoa College field evidence, West African institutional literature and carefully identified evidence from other cocoa regions.

### Evidence categories

- **Strong Ghana evidence:** current COCOBOD/CRIG guidance or peer-reviewed research conducted in Ghana.
- **Ghana field-trial evidence:** a species or system was deliberately included in a Ghana cocoa field trial. Inclusion in a trial does not by itself prove that the species is superior.
- **Ghana farmer-knowledge evidence:** peer-reviewed documentation of farmer experience, preference or reported species traits in Ghana. These observations should not be treated as experimentally measured traits unless the study measured them.
- **Historical CRIG evidence:** species or practices reported in older CRIG desirable/undesirable lists or earlier CRIG guidance. These remain relevant but should not automatically be interpreted as current policy.
- **West African supporting evidence:** research or institutional evidence from neighbouring cocoa systems, especially Côte d'Ivoire.
- **Non-Ghana regional evidence:** evidence from cocoa systems elsewhere, useful for structure or mechanisms but not sufficient on its own for Ghana species recommendations.
- **Inference:** a recommendation-system rule derived from the reviewed evidence rather than a published COCOBOD/CRIG algorithm.

A central finding across the evidence base is that **a “good companion species” is not a fixed biological category**. Suitability changes with cocoa age, rainfall zone, soil condition, existing canopy, management intensity, disease status and the farmer's economic priorities. Ghanaian farmers in a drier cocoa zone placed greater value on fruit and income trees, while farmers in a wetter zone ranked tall cocoa-compatible timber trees more highly. [S1]

## 1. Institutional context

### 1.1 COCOBOD and CRIG

COCOBOD and the Cocoa Research Institute of Ghana are the primary Ghanaian authorities for cocoa production practice and research. Current COCOBOD material distinguishes **temporary/temporal shade** from **permanent shade** and describes deliberate cocoa systems containing shade trees, fruit trees and other crops. [S3] [S6]

### 1.2 Bunso Cocoa College

Bunso Cocoa College is a **COCOBOD institution under the Cocoa Health and Extension Division**, not a CSIR institute. Its principal role is training field staff and farmers in cocoa-production technologies, although its farm has also hosted experimental research. [S18]

Two of the Ghana cocoa-*Gliricidia sepium* sites in the Kaba et al. nitrogen-fixation study were on the experimental farm of Bunso Cocoa College. [S20]

Bunso Cocoa College also offers training in plantain sucker multiplication, cassava, maize and cocoyam. The existence of those courses is **not evidence that Bunso recommends all four crops as cocoa intercrops**; cocoa-system claims for those crops must come from cocoa-specific evidence. [S29]

### 1.3 CSIR Ghana

CSIR's Plant Genetic Resources Research Institute (CSIR-PGRRI) is also located at Bunso and is a separate institution from Bunso Cocoa College. [S19]

CSIR-FORIG and CSIR-PGRRI contribute important Ghana-specific evidence on shade-tree traits, farmer preferences, participatory cocoa agroforestry trials, crown cover, functional traits and indigenous fruit-tree use. [S22] [S23] [S24] [S26] [S28]

# 2. Structure of a cocoa agroforestry system

## 2.1 Ghanaian functional structure

No reviewed COCOBOD or CRIG source establishes a single official Ghana-wide four- or five-layer height nomenclature.

The Ghana evidence instead supports the following functional structure:

1. **Temporary or temporal shade**  
   Particularly plantain during cocoa establishment or rehabilitation. [S3]

2. **Food and early intercrops**  
   Plantain, cocoyam, cassava and other establishment-stage food crops are documented in Ghana and West African cocoa systems. Their main role is during the early years rather than as permanent mature-cocoa companions. [S10] [S23]

3. **The cocoa canopy**

4. **Permanent or emergent upper-canopy trees**  
   Economic, timber, fruit and indigenous forest trees retained or planted to provide long-term regulated shade and other functions. [S3] [S6]

A COCOBOD project document explicitly distinguishes **plantain suckers providing temporal shade** from **economic tree seedlings providing permanent shade**. [S3]

Peer-reviewed Ghana research reports a CRIG interpretation of up to approximately **18 emergent trees at least 12 m high per hectare**, intended to provide roughly **30-40% permanent shade**. The same research warns that stem count alone is inadequate because tree species, diameter and crown area produce very different canopy cover. [S4]

A Ghana REDD+ decision-support report likewise emphasizes **upper-canopy cover rather than simple shade-tree counts**. [S5]

A separate Ghana landscape study involving CSIR-FORIG assessed 90 one-hectare cocoa plots across ten cocoa-growing districts. Upper-canopy stem densities were roughly 16.2-22.8 stems/ha, while measured crown cover varied from approximately 5.8% to 16.3% among districts. This supports the conclusion that stem number alone is a weak proxy for actual shade. [S28]

## 2.2 Published quantitative strata framework from Cameroon

The clearest quantitative strata framework identified in the reviewed evidence was published by Sonwa and colleagues for cocoa agroforests in southern Cameroon. It is useful as a **regional structural model**, not as an official Ghana standard. [S2]

| Published stratum | Practical interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0-5 m | Cocoa stratum and low associated plants |
| 5-10 m | Trees immediately above the cocoa canopy |
| 10-20 m | Intermediate or upper-canopy trees |
| >20 m | Emergent upper canopy |

The same study classified associated species by functions such as edible, medicinal and timber use. More than 70% of associated trees in the surveyed systems extended above the cocoa canopy. [S2]

Typical examples reported in that framework included cocoa and banana/plantain in the 0-5 m layer, fruit trees such as avocado in the 5-10 m layer, *Albizia* and *Ficus* species in the 10-20 m layer and emergent timber trees such as *Terminalia superba* above 20 m. [S2]

# 3. Criteria used to judge compatibility with cocoa

Ghanaian agronomic and farmer-knowledge literature evaluates compatibility using a combination of canopy, root, water, soil, disease, physical-risk and farmer-value criteria. [S1] [S22]

| Criterion | What is evaluated | Evidence interpretation | Potential treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shade amount and quality | Whether the crown gives adequate rather than excessive shade | Repeatedly one of the most important criteria | Strong negative if shade is too dense, low or unmanageable |
| Canopy height and architecture | Height, crown spread, openness, leaf size, branch structure and aeration | High/open crowns are often preferred | Low dense crowns can make a species unsuitable under a given spacing |
| Crown cover | Actual shade produced by crown area rather than stem count alone | Ghana evidence shows similar stem counts can produce different cover | Use canopy cover or crown class where possible |
| Phenology | Evergreen/deciduous behaviour and timing of leaf fall | Important because seasonal shade needs differ | Contextual score rather than automatic exclusion |
| Soil moisture | Reduced evaporation versus excessive water use | Especially important in drier cocoa zones | Severe drying can be a strong negative |
| Rooting and below-ground competition | Competition for water, nutrients and rooting space | Explicit criterion in Ghana farmer and agronomic literature | Strong negative where overlap is severe; no Ghana-wide numeric threshold found |
| Soil fertility | Effects on nutrient status and organic matter | Important but species- and site-dependent | Positive/negative score |
| Litter and nutrient cycling | Litter quantity, decomposition rate and nutrient return | Fast decomposition and useful nutrient return are generally favourable | Supporting agronomic score |
| Nitrogen fixation | Actual atmospheric N fixation and potential biomass-N input | Directly demonstrated in Ghana for *Gliricidia sepium* | Supporting trait; do not infer from Fabaceae membership alone |
| Pest and disease interactions | CSSVD, black pod, mirids, mistletoe, stem borers, rodents and other associations | Can outweigh otherwise positive traits | Current official phytosanitary rules may justify exclusion |
| Mechanical damage | Branch shedding, windthrow, logging and harvest damage | Repeated Ghana concern | Severe risk can make a species impractical |
| Aeration and humidity | Air movement and humidity beneath canopy | Important in wet areas and disease management | Dense stagnant canopy can require rejection or pruning |
| Wind protection | Protection without creating excessive shade/humidity | Positive service | Usually not a stand-alone exclusion |
| Weed suppression | Shade and litter reducing weed growth | Positive labour-saving service | Supporting score |
| Allelopathy | Chemical effects of tree tissues on cocoa or associated plants | Discussed in Ghana farmer-knowledge literature; no established Ghana extension exclusion rule identified | Evidence/risk flag, not a routine hard exclusion |
| Food, fruit, spice, timber or NTFP value | Household use and income diversification | Often crucial for adoption | Farmer-value score, not biological compatibility |
| Labour demand | Establishment, pruning, harvest, residue handling | Can make an otherwise compatible system impractical | Feasibility score |
| Marketability | Local demand, transport, storage and buyer access | Important for adoption and profitability | Farmer-value score |
| Seedling availability | Access to planting material and propagation | Practical implementation constraint | Can prevent implementation |
| Tree tenure/harvesting rights | Whether the farmer can benefit from the tree and control harvest damage | Particularly important for Ghana timber integration | Socioeconomic feasibility criterion |

The Ghana study summarized by Graefe et al. identified positive factors including appropriate shade, soil moisture, soil fertility, organic matter, rapid leaf decomposition, favourable microclimate, weed suppression, wind protection and aeration. Negative factors included water/nutrient competition, competition for rooting space, mechanical damage and associations with black pod, mistletoe, CSSVD, mirids, stem borers and rodents. [S1]

The CSIR-associated Atwima study documented farmer assessment of **pest/disease hosting, rooting habit, above-ground competition, allelopathy and crown characteristics**. Farmers considered crown size, branch number, leaf size and crown density because these influence solar radiation, humidity and ventilation beneath the tree. [S22]

## 3.1 Which criteria matter most?

No reviewed source provides a universal, experimentally validated Ghanaian numerical weighting system.

The strongest recurring groups are:

1. **Crown position, shade and microclimate**
2. **Pest, disease and physical risk**
3. **Water competition and site suitability**
4. **Soil and nutrient functions**
5. **Farmer value, labour and implementation feasibility**

In an Osino District study, 55% of farmer reasons for removing trees from established cocoa farms related to reducing shade and improving cocoa microclimate. [S7]

Evidence from neighbouring Côte d'Ivoire also repeatedly favoured trees with high/open crowns, useful moisture effects and relatively low water competition, while low crowns, excessive shade, competition and pest-hosting tendencies were commonly negative. [S7]

A broader cocoa analysis found low-canopy shade trees more likely to reduce cocoa yield, while elevated crowns could increase carbon with neutral yield effects. This is a useful functional-trait principle but does not replace Ghana species validation. [S8]

## 3.2 Rooting depth

Ghanaian literature clearly treats root competition as important. Farmers in the Atwima study distinguished shallow-rooted from deeper-rooted trees because shallow lateral roots were perceived as competing with cocoa for water and nutrients. [S22]

Many individual species root descriptions in that study are **farmer ecological knowledge**, not measured root profiles. Rooting evidence should therefore be tagged by source type, for example:

- experimentally measured;
- literature-derived;
- farmer-reported;
- unknown.

No reviewed Ghanaian extension source provides a universal numeric root-depth threshold for cocoa companion species. [S9]

*Terminalia ivorensis* illustrates why a binary compatibility label is insufficient: it was valued for a high, wide crown and timber, while farmers in the same Ghana study also described it as relatively shallow rooted and potentially competitive. [S22]

## 3.3 Nitrogen fixation

Nitrogen fixation should not be inferred merely because a tree belongs to Fabaceae.

The strongest Ghana-specific case identified is ***Gliricidia sepium***. Kaba et al. examined cocoa-gliricidia sites in Ghana, including two sites at Bunso Cocoa College. The system used pruned gliricidia, with shoots left beneath trees as green mulch. The study used the ^15N natural-abundance method and estimated that approximately **22-50% of gliricidia leaf nitrogen** came from atmospheric fixation, with about **48% in shoot axes**. [S20]

This demonstrates biological nitrogen fixation and a potential nutrient contribution through pruned biomass. It does **not** directly establish how much fixed nitrogen is transferred into cocoa or prove a specific cocoa-yield increase. [S20]

Independent CSIR-FORIG evidence from an eight-year-old cocoa-gliricidia system found substantially greater above-ground biomass and carbon storage when gliricidia was included, supporting its biomass-producing role. That study is biomass/carbon evidence rather than direct proof of higher cocoa bean yield. [S21]

## 3.4 Allelopathy

Allelopathy appears in Ghana farmer-knowledge literature as a possible compatibility factor. [S22]

No reviewed Ghanaian or West African extension source establishes allelopathy as a routine hard-exclusion rule. The reviewed evidence includes recent experimental work on extracts from selected shade trees, but this is not sufficient to establish a general field-level blacklist. [S12]

| Nyamedua / stoolwood | *Alstonia boonei* | Apocynaceae | Historically CRIG-desirable; included among common Ghanaian shade species in the 2022 trial; no statistically significant yield advantage over control reported in that study | Ghana field-tested + historical CRIG [S7] [S14] |

The strongest direct species-specific yield result in the reviewed material is the Ghana field study in which cocoa under ***Cedrela odorata, Khaya ivorensis, Terminalia superba* and *Milicia excelsa*** had significantly higher major-season yields than unshaded controls. That finding should not be generalized automatically to all Ghana cocoa zones, input regimes or spacings. [S14]

## 4.2 Managed service species

### *Gliricidia sepium*

- **Family:** Fabaceae
- **Roles supported by Ghana evidence:** managed/prunable shade, biological nitrogen fixation, green biomass/mulch and nutrient-cycling potential.
- **Evidence strength:** direct Ghana field research, including Bunso Cocoa College experimental sites. [S20]
- **Additional support:** CSIR-FORIG biomass/carbon evidence. [S21]
- **Limitation:** the reviewed studies do not prove a fixed quantity of nitrogen transfer to cocoa or a guaranteed cocoa-yield increase.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plantain | *Musa × paradisiaca* / plantain group | Musaceae | Temporary/temporal shade during establishment or rehabilitation; food and income | Current COCOBOD practice [S3] |

## 6.1 CRIG mixed fruit-tree experiment

CRIG tested cocoa with mixed stands containing:

- *Allanblackia parviflora*
- *Ricinodendron heudelotii*
- *Persea americana*
- *Tetrapleura tetraptera*

against a control shade mixture of *Terminalia ivorensis* and *Terminalia superba*. At 78 months, the reported experiment found **no statistically significant difference in dry cocoa-bean yield among treatments**, although canopy, ground cover, biomass and carbon differed. The researchers stated that optimal spatial arrangement had not yet been established. [S9]

These species should therefore be described as **Ghana field-tested and potentially useful multipurpose companions**, not as conclusively optimal cocoa shade species.

# 7. Additional Ghana field-tested and farmer-valued tree candidates

## 7.1 CSIR-FORIG participatory trial species

Obiri et al. established participatory cocoa agroforestry trials with farmers in Atwima, Ghana. Seven indigenous tree species were deliberately included: [S23]

- *Khaya anthotheca*
- *Pericopsis elata*
- *Entandrophragma angolense*
- *Entandrophragma utile*
- *Tetrapleura tetraptera*
- *Albizia adianthifolia*
- *Newbouldia laevis*

The establishment system also included plantain, cassava and other food crops to reflect farmer practice. [S23]

The study's principal outcome was financial analysis of shaded cocoa rather than a controlled species-by-species agronomic superiority test. These species should be labelled **Ghana participatory cocoa agroforestry trial species**, not “proven superior companions.”

# 8. Species documented as unsuitable, harmful or problematic

## 8.1 Strong phytosanitary or historical warning species

| Species | Evidence of concern | Appropriate interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| *Cola chlamydantha* | Historical CRIG undesirable list; identified as a CSSV alternative host in extension material | Strong phytosanitary warning; use current CSSVD guidance before recommendation [S7] |
| *Ceiba pentandra* | Historical CRIG-undesirable; documented CSSV alternative host; farmer reports of brittle branches, pest hosting and mechanical/logging risk | Strong disease/mechanical warning [S7] [S22] |
| *Cola gigantea* | Historical CRIG-undesirable; among least-beneficial species in later Ghana literature; farmer concerns include prolific regeneration, slow litter decomposition and dry/hard soil | Strong negative/conditional exclusion depending on site and management [S7] [S22] |
| *Musanga cecropioides* | Historical CRIG-undesirable; ranked among least-beneficial species; farmer reports of shallow roots, high water use and brittle branches | Strong Ghana negative evidence [S7] [S22] |

CSSV host status requires careful interpretation. Modern reviews confirm that wild forest hosts are biologically relevant to cacao swollen-shoot virus epidemiology, while uncertainties remain about their present importance relative to cocoa-to-cocoa transmission. A recommendation system should distinguish **confirmed host status, official removal advice and quantified epidemiological importance**. [S13]

## 8.2 Historical CRIG-undesirable species with unresolved mechanism

The reviewed historical list also contains:

- *Adansonia digitata*
- *Blighia sapida*
- *Canthium glabriflorum*
- *Carapa procera*
- *Lecaniodiscus cupanioides*
- *Myrianthus arboreus*

The reviewed source labels these species undesirable but does not provide a reliable species-by-species mechanism. They should be stored as:

**Historical CRIG classification: undesirable; mechanism unresolved in reviewed evidence.** [S7]

No root, pest, allelopathy or water-use mechanism should be invented for these species without a separate source.

## 8.3 Additional problem species from Ghana farmer knowledge

The Atwima study documented species considered aggressive or incompatible by farmers: [S22]

| Species | Problem reported by farmers |
|---|---|
| *Ceiba pentandra* | Brittle branches; reported cocoa-pest host |
| *Ficus exasperata* | Reported excessive water and nutrient use |
| *Lannea welwitschii* | Reported cocoa-pest host |
| *Musanga cecropioides* | Shallow roots, high water use, brittle branches |

This evidence is peer-reviewed documentation of Ghanaian farmer ecological knowledge, **not a current national COCOBOD “do not plant” list**.

## 8.4 Conditional rather than universally unsuitable

Several species have both positive and negative evidence:

- ***Ricinodendron heudelotii***: fruit/NTFP and income benefits, but branch shedding and heavy crowns can create mechanical risk. [S9] [S22]
- ***Persea americana*** and citrus: useful fruit/income trees, but mistletoe or pest concerns have been reported locally. [S10]
- ***Cola nitida***: income value and common retention in some systems, but historical undesirable classification and insect concerns occur in the literature. [S17] [S7]
- ***Triplochiton scleroxylon***: timber value and Ghana field evaluation, but water, branch, pest and logging risks are also reported. [S14] [S22]
- ***Milicia excelsa***: valuable and strongly supported in Ghana, but neighbouring West African farmer evidence includes local mirid concerns. [S7]
- ***Terminalia ivorensis***: high/wide crown and timber value, but farmer-reported shallow-root competition also occurs in the Ghana evidence. [S22]

A species with conflicting evidence should be labelled **conditional or evidence-conflicting**, not forced into a universal good/bad category.

# 9. Functional-trait and site evidence from CSIR Ghana

A 2024 study led by CSIR-FORIG with IITA and CIRAD collaborators compared cocoa agroforestry and monoculture systems across different soil-quality conditions and humid versus sub-humid Ghanaian environments. The researchers examined functional traits of shade-tree communities and their relationships with soil and ecosystem services. [S26] [S27]

The study found strong environmental context effects: initial soil quality explained a substantial part of soil-related outcomes, while shade-tree functional traits helped predict ecosystem services. [S26] [S27]

The resulting evidence supports a selection sequence based on:

**farm/location -> cocoa stage -> site conditions -> required function -> species traits -> exclusions/risks -> farmer/economic value -> recommendation**

rather than:

**cocoa present -> recommend a fixed tree species**

| Finding | Geographic basis | Use in Ghana recommendations |

| Yield response of eight common shade species | Three-year study on 10 farms in Western Ghana | Strong local evidence; do not automatically extrapolate nationwide [S14] |
| CRIG fruit-tree mixture experiment | Ghana | Ghana-specific field evidence; optimal arrangement not established [S9] |
| Atwima farmer trait/preferences and incompatibility evidence | Ghana | Strong evidence of local farmer knowledge; distinguish from measured traits [S22] |

| African star apple use in cocoa | Eastern Ghana | Ghana farmer-use/income evidence [S24] [S25] |
| Functional-trait/soil-context study | Ghana | Direct support for context-sensitive selection [S26] [S27] |
| 0-5, 5-10, 10-20 and >20 m strata | Southern Cameroon | Useful structural framework, not Ghana extension standard [S2] |
| High/open crowns and low competition | West African regional evidence | Useful trait-level support; local validation still required [S7] |
| Global cocoa agroforestry effects | Multi-region meta-analysis | Useful for decision dimensions, not Ghana species rankings [S11] |

The global cocoa meta-analysis emphasizes that agroforestry outcomes vary with ecosystem, soils, climate, management, planting material, markets, farmer knowledge and land-use history. Disease outcomes also depend on the pathogen and microclimate. [S11]

This limits direct transfer from Latin America or Southeast Asia. Diseases such as frosty pod rot and witches' broom are not the same disease problem as Ghana's CSSVD and black pod complex. A shade or species relationship observed for one pathogen should not automatically be transferred to another. [S11]

Some species used in Ghana cocoa systems, including *Gliricidia sepium*, *Cedrela odorata* and *Persea americana*, originate outside West Africa. Their inclusion in this evidence base rests on documented Ghana or West African use/testing, not simply on research from their native ranges. [S7] [S15] [S20]

# 11. Decision framework for companion and intercrop selection

The reviewed sources do not provide a single published COCOBOD/CRIG algorithm. The following framework is an evidence-based operationalization of the literature.

## Stage 1: Safety and hard screens

Exclude, block or require specialist review where there is:

- current official phytosanitary removal guidance;
- a confirmed disease-host concern relevant to an affected CSSVD rehabilitation context;
- severe branch-failure, windthrow or logging risk that cannot be managed;
- an unmanageable low/dense crown under the intended spacing;
- invasive or excessively aggressive regeneration;
- no legal right for the farmer to manage or benefit from the tree;
- no credible planting material or feasible management method.

Historical undesirable status alone should normally be stored as a warning/evidence label unless it is supported by current official guidance.

## Stage 2: Farm context

Evaluate:

1. **Cocoa stage:** establishment, immature, mature, old/rehabilitation.
2. **Agroecological context:** rainfall/humidity zone and temperature.
3. **Soil condition:** pH, texture, organic carbon, nitrogen and other available soil indicators.
4. **Water balance:** rainfall, evapotranspiration and drought/water-deficit conditions.
5. **Existing canopy cover:** actual shade/crown cover rather than stem number alone.
6. **Disease status:** especially CSSVD and major cocoa disease/pest pressure.
7. **Existing companions:** species already present and their density/arrangement.

## Stage 3: Species compatibility

Score each candidate on:

1. expected mature height;
2. crown area and openness;
3. leaf size/density and phenology;
4. capacity to provide appropriate rather than excessive shade;
5. rooting and water competition;
6. litter production and decomposition;
7. nutrient-cycling evidence;
8. nitrogen fixation where directly supported;
9. known pest, disease, mistletoe and rodent associations;
10. mechanical/wind risk;
11. pruning and management burden;
12. interaction with existing companions.

## Stage 4: Farmer-value and implementation fit

Evaluate separately from biological compatibility:

1. food value;
2. fruit/spice/NTFP value;
3. timber value;
4. local marketability;
5. household use;
6. labour requirement;
7. seedling availability;
8. time to economic benefit;
9. tree tenure/harvesting rights;
10. farmer objective and risk tolerance.

## Stage 5: Evidence confidence

Every species recommendation should preserve:

- evidence geography;
- evidence type;
- study/practice source;
- whether the trait was measured, farmer-reported or inferred;
- whether evidence is consistent or conflicting;
- whether the result applies to establishment, mature cocoa or both.

---

# 12. Recommended evidence labels

Use explicit evidence labels rather than a bare “recommended” tag:

- **Official Ghana establishment practice**
- **Ghana field-tested**
- **Ghana participatory cocoa trial**
- **Ghana farmer-practice evidence**
- **Ghana farmer-knowledge trait**
- **Historical CRIG desirable**
- **Historical CRIG undesirable**
- **West African neighbouring evidence**
- **Evidence conflicting**
- **Mechanism uncertain**
- **Non-Ghana cocoa evidence only**
- **No reliable source found**

Examples of appropriately qualified outputs:

> **Plantain:** Suitable as temporary shade during cocoa establishment/rehabilitation; official Ghana practice. Intended role is temporary shade and early food/income rather than permanent mature-cocoa canopy.

> **Terminalia superba:** Strong Ghana permanent-shade/timber candidate with direct major-season yield evidence; final suitability depends on existing canopy, site and management.

> **Ricinodendron heudelotii:** Multipurpose fruit/NTFP candidate with Ghana evidence, but branch-shedding and heavy-crown risks require spacing and canopy management.

> **Triplochiton scleroxylon:** Evidence conflicting. Timber and field-use evidence exist, but Ghana farmer knowledge reports water, mechanical and pest concerns.

# 14. Core conclusions

1. **There is no single universal “best cocoa companion” list for Ghana.** Species suitability depends on cocoa stage, site, soil, water, existing canopy, disease and management. [S1] [S26]

2. **Ghana's cocoa agroforestry structure is best represented functionally** as temporary shade/early intercrops, cocoa canopy and permanent upper-canopy trees. Sonwa's four height bands are a useful regional framework but are not a Ghana standard. [S2] [S3]

3. **Canopy architecture and actual crown cover are more informative than tree count alone.** [S4] [S22] [S28]

4. **Pest/disease risk, water competition and severe mechanical risk can outweigh economic or shade benefits.** [S1] [S22]

5. **No reviewed source provides a validated Ghana-wide numerical weighting system or root-depth threshold.**

6. **Nitrogen fixation is a supporting trait, not an automatic recommendation rule.** Direct Ghana evidence exists for *Gliricidia sepium*, but Fabaceae membership alone is insufficient. [S20]

7. **Plantain has the clearest current official role as temporary shade in young/re-established cocoa.** [S3]

8. **The strongest direct Ghana yield evidence among reviewed permanent shade species supports *Cedrela odorata*, *Khaya ivorensis*, *Terminalia superba* and *Milicia excelsa* in the specific Western Ghana field study.** [S14]

9. **Fruit and multipurpose trees should be evaluated for both cocoa compatibility and farmer value.** CRIG and CSIR evidence supports several candidates, but economic usefulness does not override serious agronomic risk. [S9] [S22] [S24]

10. **Historical CRIG undesirable lists should not be converted into unexplained modern bans.** Preserve historical status, current disease evidence and unresolved mechanisms separately. [S7]

11. **Farmer ecological knowledge is valuable but must be labelled correctly.** A reported deep root, pest association or soil effect should not be presented as an experimentally measured botanical trait unless it was measured. [S22]

12. **The defensible recommendation model is trait- and context-based:**  
   **farm/location -> cocoa stage -> site conditions -> existing canopy -> disease/safety screens -> required function -> species traits -> farmer/economic fit -> evidence-labelled recommendation.**
